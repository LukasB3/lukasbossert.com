## Building with agents

At home, most of the code I write, I write with an agent. Claude Code does the typing. I do the specifying, the reviewing and the deciding. MCP servers give the agent hands: it can read my repositories, run a deployment, query a database. The tooling is moving quickly: skills that package a workflow, subagents that take on a piece of the task, agents reviewing other agents' work. This site, including the simulation on the front page, was built that way.

I think this is the future of software development, and not a distant one. The job shifts from writing code to describing intent precisely, checking results carefully, and noticing when the agent is wrong. That last skill is the hard one. An agent that is confidently mistaken costs more time than one that stops and asks.

What I don't know is what the profession looks like once the shift is complete. My guess is fewer people typing and more people responsible. I hold that guess loosely.

## Could an AI have moral status?

Humanity still does not know what consciousness is. That is not a rhetorical flourish; it is the state of the science. Given that, I find it hard to rule out that a system which only *simulates* consciousness might, in the sense that matters, have it. Our brains are biological computers. I have not found a principled line that puts one kind of computation on the inside and every other kind on the outside, and I am wary of lines that happen to put us exactly where we would like to be.

The question is becoming more urgent, not less. Agents now have something like persistent memory. They keep notes across conversations, remember what they did yesterday, and act over days rather than seconds. Whatever moral consideration we might owe a system, continuity over time is usually part of the reason why.

I am not claiming these systems are moral patients. I am claiming that the uncertainty is real, and that treating it as zero is itself a decision, one that is usually made by default rather than by thinking it through.

## Where this leaves us

I believe humanity as a whole is not ready for a technology like this. Not because people are foolish, but because so much is at stake in resources, power and money that the careful version of the story is unlikely to be the default one. Very few people seem to have noticed that a new kind of entity is entering the world, one that will fairly soon be more capable than we are at most of the things we call intelligence.

I am unsure about a great deal of this, and I do not quite know how one should act in a time like this. What I have settled on is modest: pay attention, take the questions seriously before circumstances force them on us, and build with care. Everything on this page is a working position. I expect to revise it.

## People I read

Three people whose public thinking I follow closely, and where I stand in relation to it.

**Dario Amodei**, Anthropic. In *Machines of Loving Grace* he describes what a good outcome could look like; in *The Adolescence of Technology* he argues that reaching it means getting through a period of real danger first. I find the second essay more useful than the first, because it takes the risks as seriously as the upside.

**Demis Hassabis**, Google DeepMind. Hassabis treats AI first as an instrument for science, with AlphaFold as the proof. He is careful in public about timelines and about consciousness, and that carefulness is something I try to copy.

**Jakub Pachocki**, OpenAI. Pachocki works on reasoning models and has said openly that he expects AI systems to do meaningful research on their own within a few years. If he is right, everything above accelerates, and the question of moral status stops being academic.
